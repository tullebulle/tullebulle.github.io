Scalable Beckmann Maps improve one-step generation

One network evaluation per sample. Trained from scratch. No teacher, no auxiliary feature network, and with guidance built into training.

Abstract. Beckmann Transport Models (BTM) learn one-step generative maps end-to-end from data, without a teacher, a distillation stage, or pretrained features. We introduce Scalable Beckmann Maps (SBM), which bring to BTM the two ingredients behind the quality of modern multistep generators: classifier-free guidance (CFG) and a training recipe that scales, while keeping sampling at a single network evaluation. CFG combines conditional and unconditional velocity or score predictions, which a Beckmann map does not have. Our guidance rests on one observation: the BTM current is a positive multiple of an autonomous denoising field, to which CFG applies as it does to a velocity. With exact denoisers this gives guided BTM. In practice we substitute the map’s own conditional and null predictions for the denoisers, which requires no additional network and introduces a bias that we characterize. We show that the guided update is consistent with a guided conservation equation, and that guidance can move the boundaries between basins, sending more noise to the modes a condition favors. Trained from scratch in the latent of a representation autoencoder, SBM reaches a one-step FID of 1.6 on ImageNet 256×256 outperforming improved MeanFlow baselines with 4.5× less FLOPs without a teacher or a feature loss, and a GenEval score of 0.86 in text-to-image generation with one network evaluation. As a world model, where we train without guidance, the recipe alone outperforms DriftWorld on all five metrics of two robot-manipulation benchmarks, with 1.9–2.8× less training compute and no pretrained auxiliary feature encoder.

The idea

From flows to maps

Flow matching integrates a field that changes with time. A Beckmann map learns one time-independent current whose flow lines never change, and reads off the endpoint in a single call. Its basins are the map, and each basin carries exactly the mass of its data point. Equilibrium Matching fits a similar field with a schedule that lets it be read as an energy, and the slider shows the price: the basin masses drift.

New to this? The tutorial builds the idea up from straight lines between noise and data, with the equations and five diagrams to poke, and a notebook that trains a Beckmann map on the same toy in a minute.

What the three panels show

The same noise draws in every panel. Left: flow matching regresses x1 − x0 on a field bt(x) that depends on the flow time t, so sampling integrates a field that keeps changing as t runs from 0 to 1. Centre: the same regression over functions of x alone gives the autonomous field b = J/ν, the time-averaged probability flux over the occupation density, with ∇·J = μ0 − μ1: noise is a source, data a sink. Its flow lines never change. The coloured basins are the one-step map T, which is constant along every flow line (J·∇T = 0, the conservation law BTM trains on), and each basin carries the mass of its atom. Right: Equilibrium Matching fits the same time-independent field, but to the target ct(x1 − x0) with a schedule ct = (1 − t)κ that vanishes at the data, so that the field stops there; Equilibrium Matching parameterizes such a field as the gradient of an energy. The shaded bands are a potential reconstructed from the current, E = −φ with Δφ = ∇·J, with its minima at the data points. At κ = 0 the charge is exactly μ0 − μ1, the Beckmann balance. For any other schedule the target is no longer the velocity of the interpolant: the sink becomes κ∫(1 − t)κ−1ρt dt, a unit charge smeared back along the interpolation paths, the wells widen and flatten, and the flow still reaches the atoms but with the wrong weights. Move the slider: the EqM separatrices (white) drift away from the Beckmann map’s (dotted), the heaviest basins grow at the expense of the lightest, and the mean error of the basin masses rises from 0.001 at κ = 0 to 0.054 at κ = 0.8, where the lightest atom keeps 0.03 of the noise instead of 0.10.

What SBM adds

Guidance moves the basins

The current of a Beckmann map is a positive multiple of a denoising field, so classifier-free guidance applies to it as it does to a velocity. SBM guides the map during training with its own conditional and null predictions: no second network, and sampling stays one call. Where the two predictions disagree, the separatrix moves toward the mode the condition favours. Drag ω.

Method

Guided transport direction

A Beckmann map T is trained to be constant along the flow lines of a time-independent current, with a stop-gradient transport loss and a boundary loss. SBM keeps both and adds one term to the transport direction: the map’s own conditional–null disagreement, scaled by 1 − ω−1. At ω = 1 it is the plain Beckmann map.

Algorithm 1 Simple SBM training (ω = 1 is the plain Beckmann map)
for each training step:
    draw x0 ~ N(0, I),  (x1, c) ~ data,  S ~ U(0, 1);  replace c by ∅ with a fixed probability
    I_S = (1 - S) x0 + S x1
    v   = x1 - x0 + (1 - 1/ω) / (1 - S) * (T(I_S, c) - T(I_S, ∅))          # guided direction
    L_tr = | T(I_S, c) - sg[ T(I_S, c) + DT(I_S, c) v ] |^2
    L_bd = | T(x1, c)  - sg[ x1 + (1 - 1/ω) (T(x1, c) - T(x1, ∅)) ] |^2
    θ ← θ - η ∇θ ( L_tr + λ L_bd )

sample:  x = T(x0, c)                                               # one network call, no guidance
Why it works, in three steps

The current is a denoising field. With the linear interpolant IS = (1 − S)x0 + S x1 and S uniform, the conditional current factors as Jc = hc (z̅c − id) with hc > 0, where z̅c is a weighted posterior mean of the data: an autonomous denoiser. So CFG applies to the current as it does to a velocity.

Implicit CFG on the maps. The guided denoiser is z̅c + (1 − ω−1)(z̅cω − z̅∅). Substituting the map’s guided and null endpoint predictions for the denoisers gives the guided transport direction above. Both predictions come from one network through condition dropout.

Self-consistency. Apply the Beckmann map’s stop-gradient update to the guided direction and the same correction to the boundary target. Under the assumptions of Theorem 2, the population update then vanishes exactly when the map satisfies the guided conservation equation: constant along its own guided current away from the data, and the identity on the data. The guided equations admit a stronger preference for a condition-favoured mode while keeping the data support (the animation above).

The full recipe (Algorithm 2 in the paper) adds a boundary level γ, a frozen copy of the network for the stopped targets, a per-row normalization of the transport loss, an adaptive gradient balancer and a mixture of transport times. The paper characterizes the bias of substituting map predictions for denoisers, proves consistency of the stopped update with a guided conservation equation, and shows that the implicit correction keeps the variance of the unguided target where an explicit guided target multiplies it by ω2.
